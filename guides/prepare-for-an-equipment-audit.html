Preparing for an equipment audit: the evidence you need and who holds it

Published 2026-11-26. Written by Trackora. The example in this article is fictional.

An equipment audit is often decided before anyone counts a device. Can you show the purchase, the approval, the assignment and the disposal? Preparation means finding that evidence while there is still time to fix the gaps. Completing it does not guarantee an audit result, and requirements vary by organisation, jurisdiction and audit scope.

Agree scope and owners first

Write down the objective in a sentence or two, which equipment, locations and period are in scope, and what is deliberately out. Name an audit sponsor, a preparation lead, and an owner for each kind of evidence: finance, procurement, IT, HR, facilities.

List evidence by control area

Ten areas cover most equipment audits: policies; purchase documentation; supplier records; approvals; assignment and return; transfers; disposal; access and responsibility for changing records; historical evidence such as earlier counts; and how exceptions are handled. For each, list the documents an auditor could ask for.

Track every item

Give each item an owner and a due date, then track its state: not requested, requested, received, under review, accepted or incomplete. Note exactly what is missing. In the fictional example, 15 items are tracked and, mid-March, four are overdue, one is due soon and three have missing information. That is a short list to chase, not a vague worry.

Trace a small sample end to end

Pick a few items and follow each from approval to purchase, receipt, tagging, assignment, any transfer, and return or disposal. A handful of traced items usually shows where the chain breaks.

Record exceptions as findings

Equipment assigned to someone who has left, purchases without a recorded approval, devices found that are not in the register: record each with a risk level, a root cause, a recommended action, one owner and a target date. Close a finding only with verification evidence. In the example, nine findings include two overdue actions and two open high-risk items.

Report in numbers

For management, report scope, the number of evidence items received and missing, findings by risk, overdue actions and the decisions you need. Numbers are easier to act on than adjectives.

Counting is separate

This is about paperwork and controls. A physical count compares what is in the building with the register. You need both.
