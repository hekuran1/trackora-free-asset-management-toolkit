How to build an IT equipment budget in an afternoon

Published 2026-11-26. Written by Trackora. The example in this article is fictional.

An equipment budget is easy to start and easy to get wrong. The usual failure is double counting: the estimate for a purchase and its final invoice both end up in the total, so the budget looks fuller than it is. This guide shows a simple method that avoids that, with a worked example you can copy.

1. Start with departments, not items

Write down each department and its approved allocation. Even a rough figure works, because you will adjust it. In our fictional example, a company of ten departments has an approved equipment budget of 321,000 for the year, ranging from 7,000 for Legal to 90,000 for Engineering.

2. List purchases one row at a time

For each planned purchase record the department, category, quantity, and an estimated unit cost taken from a recent quote or invoice. One row per purchase is easier to defend in a review than one number per department, because a manager can approve or reject a row.

3. Give every row a status

Use a short list: Draft, Submitted, Approved, Ordered, Received, Rejected, Cancelled. Decide which statuses count toward the budget. Rejected and Cancelled rows should not, but they stay visible so nobody proposes the same purchase twice.

4. Replace the estimate with the actual cost

When you buy, type the actual unit cost. From that moment the row should count at its actual total, not its estimate. In the example, twelve developer laptops were estimated at 2,100 each and bought at 2,050, so the row drops from 25,200 to 24,600 and the variance of minus 600 shows you estimated high.

5. Measure committed spend, not just spend

Committed spend is the actual cost where you have it and the estimate where you do not. It is the honest figure to compare with the allocation. In the example it is 177,060 of 321,000, or 55 percent, so the organisation can see how much room is left before the year is half over.

6. Look for the departments that need a conversation

Flag a department when it passes a warning level such as 90 percent, and again when it goes over. In the example Marketing is at 99 percent of its allocation and Legal is at 103 percent because four laptops cost 7,200 against a 7,000 budget. That is a useful conversation to have in February, not in November.

7. Spread the spend across months and test price rises

Place each purchase in the month you expect to pay. Spending that is bunched into two months is a cash-flow question as much as a budget question. Then ask what a price rise would do. Apply it to the purchases you have not yet made, because money already spent does not change. In the example, ten percent higher prices add about 9,650 to committed spend.

Keep it honest

A budget workbook only calculates what you type. It does not predict market prices or fetch supplier quotes, and a scenario percentage is an assumption, not a forecast. Review the figures with the people who buy the equipment.
